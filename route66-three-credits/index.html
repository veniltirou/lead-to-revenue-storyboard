One Deal, Three Credits — The Route 66 Automobiles Story

Chapter 3 followed a single deal from Closed Won to a paycheck, through one person's plan. This chapter asks a harder question: when three different people all had a hand in the same deal, who gets paid, and how much? The answer is more surprising than it sounds — the total credit awarded can exceed the deal's own value, and that isn't a bug.

Business Scenario: James closes ABC Corporation's largest deal of the year with Route 66 Automobiles, a dealership group — vending machines across showroom floors, service waiting areas and staff break rooms at eleven sites. It's a $150,000 contract. But James didn't close it alone. Sarah manages him. Jeff was brought in as the multi-site specialist who designed the rollout. All three earn from this one transaction, under three different plans, by three different rules.
A note on the numbers: The credit percentages, rates and resulting earnings in this chapter follow Oracle's own instructor-led training material for Fusion Incentive Compensation. The three-way split shown here — direct, rollup and overlay on a single transaction — is the standard pattern Oracle uses to teach why transactions and credits are kept as separate objects. Every figure has been checked to reconcile.

Scene 1 — The Biggest Deal of the Year

What this shows: James marks the Route 66 Automobiles opportunity Closed Won in Oracle CX Sales — $150,000 for vending across the dealer group's showrooms, service lounges and staff break rooms. It is ABC's largest single order this year. The transaction flows into Oracle Incentive Compensation automatically, and from this point on the interesting question is no longer whether it closed, but who gets paid for it.

Scene 2 — Three People, One Deal

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: James closed it, but he wasn't alone. Sarah Johnson is his sales manager. Jeff is ABC's multi-site solutions specialist, brought in to design the rollout across eleven Route 66 locations. All three will be paid on this deal. Understanding why each of them is credited, and why the amounts differ so sharply, is what the rest of this chapter is about.

Scene 3 — Transaction, Credit and Earning Are Three Different Things

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: The distinction everything else depends on, and the most common source of confusion in Incentive Compensation. A transaction is the deal itself ($150,000). A credit is an award to a specific person for that deal. An earning is what a credit becomes once a formula has run against it. They are three separate objects — and the reason they are separate is precisely because multiple people can be credited for one transaction. If credit were simply a field on the deal, only one person could ever be paid.

Scene 4 — Direct Credit — James

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: James owns the Route 66 opportunity and closed it, so he receives 100% Direct Credit — the full $150,000. His Enterprise Sales Plan pays 8%, giving him $12,000. Direct credit is the simplest of the three types and the one most people picture when they think of commission. Note that it is not a share of a pool: James is credited with the entire deal value, and crediting other people does nothing to reduce it.

Scene 5 — Rollup Credit — Sarah

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: Sarah did not close this deal. She is credited because she manages James — rollup credit flows up the management line automatically whenever anyone in the team sells. She too receives 100% credit, the same full $150,000, but her Management Override Plan pays a deliberately low 1.5%, giving $2,250. The credit reflects that the deal happened in her team; the rate reflects that she did not sell it herself. Changing either changes the message the plan sends.

Scene 6 — Overlay Credit — Jeff

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: Jeff is not James's manager and does not report to him — he sits beside the sales line rather than above it. He was brought in for his expertise in multi-site deployments, and overlay credit exists for exactly this: specialists who contribute to deals they will never own. He receives 50% — $75,000, a partial award, at the highest rate of the three, 10%, giving $7,500. He earns more than Sarah despite being credited with half as much, because ABC wants specialists genuinely motivated to support deals that will never carry their name.

The distinction worth holding onto: Sarah is credited because she is above James in the hierarchy. Jeff is credited because he worked alongside him. Same deal, different relationships, different credit types, different plans.

Scene 7 — The Numbers Don't Add Up — and That Is Correct

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: Add the credits together: $150,000 + $150,000 + $75,000 = $375,000 of credit awarded on a $150,000 deal — 250% of its value. This looks wrong and isn't. Oracle tracks each credit independently because each one feeds a different person's plan; credit was never meant to sum to the deal value. What matters commercially is not total credit but total cost: $12,000 + $2,250 + $7,500 = $21,750, or 14.5% of the deal. That is the number ABC budgets against, and confusing the two is the most common misreading of an incentive compensation report.

Scene 8 — Three Plans, Three Formulas

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: Each person's credit lands on a different plan, and the rates encode ABC's intent rather than being arbitrary. James's 8% rewards closing. Sarah's 1.5% acknowledges leadership without paying her as though she sold it. Jeff's 10% is deliberately the highest rate on the smallest credit base. The two levers work independently: credit percentage decides how much of the deal counts for someone, while rate decides what that is worth to them. ABC could halve Jeff's credit to 25% and double his rate to 20% and he would earn exactly the same $7,500 — the arithmetic identical, the message to the participant quite different.

Scene 9 — The Nine Stages

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: The complete lifecycle, and where crediting sits within it. Stages ① Design and ② Assign happened before the deal existed — the plans were configured and the three participants placed on them. Everything from ③ Import onward is triggered by the transaction itself: it is classified, credited three ways, each participant's attainment is updated, formulas run, and paysheets are released. Stage ⑨ Dispute is the loop back, if anyone disagrees with what they see.

Scene 10 — Lisa Explains the Design

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: Lisa, ABC's compensation administrator, explains the reasoning to James at the whiteboard. Crediting rules are configured once and then applied automatically to every transaction that qualifies — which is what makes the whole arrangement scalable. Without separate credit objects, ABC would need either three copies of every transaction or a manual process to work out who earned what, and both fall apart at volume.

Scene 11 — The Whole Flow

📱 On mobile? Pinch to zoom to read the details clearly.

What this shows: Everything in one view. The $150,000 transaction arrives, fans into three credits, each meets a different plan at a different rate, and the three earnings converge on a total cost of $21,750. One sale, three people paid, three sets of rules — and a total that remains a sensible fraction of the deal's value.

Why This Chapter Matters

The takeaway: the separation of transaction, credit and earning is not bureaucratic layering — it is what makes team selling possible to compensate at all. Once you see that a credit is an award to a person rather than a property of the deal, the arithmetic that looked broken becomes obvious: three people were credited because three people contributed, and each was paid according to a plan written for the part they played. The figure that alarms people ($375,000 of credit) measures contribution. The figure that matters ($21,750 of cost) measures spend. They were never the same number.

Concepts Covered

ConceptWhat it meansScene
TransactionThe deal itself — one record of one sale, with a value, a customer and a date, but no owner yet. It is the input to crediting, not the thing anyone is paid on directly.3
CreditAn award to a specific person for a transaction. This is where a deal acquires an owner — and because credit is its own object rather than a field on the deal, one transaction can produce several credits to several people.3
EarningWhat a credit becomes once a formula has run against it. The same credit meeting different plans produces different earnings, which is why three people credited on one deal are paid three different amounts.3
Direct CreditCredit to the rep who owns and closes the opportunity, normally 100% of the deal value. It is not a share of a pool: crediting others does nothing to reduce it.4
Rollup CreditCredit that flows up the management hierarchy automatically whenever anyone in the team sells. The manager is credited with the full deal value but paid at a much lower rate, acknowledging leadership without paying as though they had sold it.5
Overlay CreditCredit for a specialist who supports a deal from outside the selling rep's management line — typically a partial percentage at a high rate, so that specialists are motivated to support deals they will never own.6
Credit independenceEach participant is calculated separately against their own plan. Rollup and overlay credit do not reduce the closing rep's direct credit, and none of the three competes with the others.4–7
Total credit vs total costCredit can exceed the transaction value — here $375,000 of credit on a $150,000 deal. Credit measures contribution; only the sum of the earnings ($21,750) measures what the company actually spends.7
Credit percentage and rateThe two independent levers of plan design. Credit percentage decides how much of a deal counts for someone; rate decides what that is worth. The same earning can be produced by very different combinations, each sending a different message.8
Multiple plans per transactionOne transaction can be processed against several compensation plans simultaneously, each with its own measures, formula and rate table.5, 6, 8
Crediting rulesConfigured once and applied automatically to every qualifying transaction — a direct rule for the owning rep, a rollup rule that walks the management hierarchy, and overlay rules for named specialists.4–6, 10
The nine-stage lifecycleDesign, Assign, Import, Classify, Credit, Measure, Calculate, Payment, Dispute. The first two are configured in advance; the rest are triggered by a transaction arriving.9
