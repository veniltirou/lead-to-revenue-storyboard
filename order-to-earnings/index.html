Advanced · Incentive Compensation

From Order to Earnings — One Order, Fifteen Credits, One Paycheck

A $60,000 order arrives, and by the end of the evening Incentive Compensation has turned it into fifteen credits worth $180,000, and $1,200 of earnings for a junior rep. This chapter puts the whole run on a projector: collect, credit, roll up, classify and calculate, one step at a time, through the eyes of the person it pays. It builds on One Deal, Three Credits, which explains why total credit can exceed a deal, and Three Transactions, One Paycheck, which maps the objects along the way.

Business Scenario: Mile High City Bank, one of ABC Corporation's Denver accounts, opens a new branch and orders three products: a snack vending machine ($12,000), a beverage vending machine ($18,000) and a micro market ($30,000), $60,000 in all. James Mitchell owns the account, and Leila, now a junior sales rep, works it with him. Lisa, ABC's compensation analyst, puts the order on the projector and walks Leila through what happens to it.

The split is already set. On ABC's Denver Multi-Site credit rule, James receives 60% and Leila 40% of each line as revenue credit; Jeff, the multi-site specialist, receives 100% overlay credit; and Sarah Johnson, their sales manager, receives rollup credit from the parent rule. ABC classifies after crediting and rollup, just before calculation.
A note on accuracy: the processing order, the two classification settings, how crediting creates one credit per receiver on the winning rule, how rollup mirrors direct credits, and how a credit's category must match a plan's measure all follow Oracle's documentation. The company, the order, the rule names, the VENDING parent category and the 5% rate are illustration, but every number reconciles.

Prologue — Lisa Takes the Projector

What this shows: the question the rest of the chapter answers. Leila has co-sold her first big order and wants to know what it is worth to her. Rather than quote a number, Lisa puts the whole journey on the screen, order, credit, earnings, because the number only makes sense once you have seen the steps that produce it.

Scene 1 — Mile High Orders for Branch 15

What this shows: James sets down the signed order, three lines for Mile High's newest branch, $60,000 in all, and hands Leila to Lisa. Before anything runs, Lisa states the split: James 60% of every line, Leila 40%. The split is not stored on either of them. It sits on ABC's Denver Multi-Site credit rule, decided by leadership and configured by Lisa, and every order that matches that rule is split the same way.

Scene 2 — Seven Steps, One Order

What this shows: the whole route before any of it is magnified: import, collect, credit, roll up, classify, calculate, pay. Leila's question is the right one, because classification is the one step whose position is a choice. Oracle offers two settings for When to Classify Transactions: after collection and before crediting, or after crediting and rollup and before calculation. ABC uses the second. Oracle advises choosing once and leaving it, because switching means reprocessing every open period.

Scene 3 — Import and Collect

What this shows: the order is imported to staging and collected into three base transactions, BT-1001 to BT-1003, one per order line. ABC loads its lines separately so each product can be credited and classified on its own. Lisa starts everything with Run All Transaction Processes in the Credits and Earnings work area, which also deploys the latest credit and classification rules before it uses them.

Scene 4 — Credit

What this shows: all three lines match the Denver Multi-Site rule, so crediting creates one credit for each receiver on that rule, for each line: James 60% ($36,000), Leila 40% ($24,000) and Jeff 100% overlay ($60,000). That is nine direct credits. Jeff's count as direct because he is named on the rule itself. Leila's worry is the natural one, and the answer is the same as in the Route 66 story: Jeff's credit is marked Nonrevenue and sits on top of the reps' shares. It takes nothing from her 40%.

Scene 5 — Roll Up

What this shows: Sarah is a credit receiver on the parent rule. Rollup gives her one credit for every direct credit whose receiver has Roll Up to Parents set to Yes, James's three and Leila's three, each mirroring the amount it came from: six credits, $60,000. Jeff's rows are set to No, so nothing rolls up from his overlay. Three order lines have now become fifteen credits.

What this shows: add them up and one $60,000 order has produced $180,000 of credit: $120,000 direct ($36,000 + $24,000 + $60,000) and $60,000 rollup. As in Route 66, total credit was never meant to equal the deal. Only the revenue credits split the sale; overlay and rollup recognize the same sale toward other people's goals, without taking anything from the reps.

Scene 6 — Classify

What this shows: with crediting and rollup done, Classify Credits runs against all fifteen credits. ABC's classification rules map each product to a credit category, SNACK, BEVERAGE or MICRO_MARKET, all children of a parent category, VENDING. Every credit on a line gets that line's category, Jeff's and Sarah's included: five per line. Classifying after crediting is the setting that would let a category differ by credit receiver if ABC ever needed that; today, it doesn't.

Scene 7 — Calculate

What this shows: calculation only counts a credit whose category matches a performance measure on the participant's plan, or is a child of the measure's category. Leila's Vending Revenue measure uses VENDING, the parent of all three, so all three of her credits match and move from Classified to Eligible: $24,000 × 5% = $1,200. A credit that matched no measure would earn nothing until it was fixed. Then Pay runs, and Leila's payment plan turns her earnings into a paycheck.

Why This Chapter Matters

The takeaway: your split decides your credit, and classification connects that credit to the plan that pays it. One $60,000 order became fifteen credits worth $180,000 and, for Leila, $1,200 of earnings, and every one of those numbers traces back to a rule someone set up before the order arrived.

Most confusion about pay is a question nobody answered before payday. Why does Jeff have $60,000 of credit? Why does Sarah have six credits? Why is my number $1,200? Lisa answers all three with the projector on, before the statement arrives. The lesson for sales ops: explain the credits before the paycheck, not after.
